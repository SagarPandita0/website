DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on one DGX Spark: making 510 GB work

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash is far larger than the Spark's 128 GB of unified memory. The interesting part is that most of its mixture-of-experts weights are not needed at the same instant. A custom research engine uses that fact in two ways: keep the frequently chosen FP4 experts in memory and fetch the rest from SSD, or keep a smaller, 3-bit set of experts entirely resident.

I ran both strategies on one DGX Spark and measured generation speed, first-token latency, expert hit rate, physical SSD traffic, held-out next-token quality, routing coverage, memory pressure, thermals, clocks, and power. This page is about the mechanism and its trade-off—not a claim that the full checkpoint simply fits in memory.

the idea in plain english

A normal dense model uses every feed-forward block for every token. This model instead has 384 small specialist blocks—“experts”—in each of 40 layers and routes each token through only six. That leaves room for an unusual memory hierarchy.

Memory bars: green is the always-resident model core, blue or orange is the expert arena, and gray is working headroom.

The full-FP4 path is the faithful one: no expert is removed, and every expert keeps its native FP4 representation. An 81 GB arena holds 3,910 hot experts plus a 400-slot transient ring. A miss triggers a direct read from local NVMe. The transient ring is important during prompt processing, when many experts appear briefly; those one-off reads do not displace the hot decode set.

The faster path starts with a routing trace over 10,760 tokens. It keeps 154 of the 384 experts in every layer, then repacks those 6,160 experts into a per-row 3-bit codebook format called CB3. They occupy about 89 GB and stay resident. That removes expert SSD reads, but it also changes the model: 60% of the experts can no longer be selected, and the remaining expert weights are more compressed.

The trade-off: FP4 streaming preserves the expert set but waits on storage. CB3 removes that wait and is 2.5–3.3× faster here, at a measurable next-token quality cost.

The model checkpoint was 510,313,423,455 bytes and all 48 indexed weight shards were validated before the run. The software stack used PyTorch 2.13.0+cu130, Transformers 5.17.0, Triton 3.7.1, and the custom engine at commit 8bb38e1.

what I would take from this

  • The model can run on one Spark, but storage is part of the computer. Full FP4 generation is practical only because the engine treats unified memory and NVMe as one managed hierarchy.
  • Expert locality is real enough to exploit. A 28% FP4 resident arena produced 82–89% measured expert hit rates on the three serving workloads.
  • All-resident CB3 changes the bottleneck. It eliminated expert reads and lifted decode to 13.28–26.88 tok/s, but used nearly all available memory.
  • The speedup has a quality price. The matched held-out test lost 2.01 top-one points on code and 2.64 on general text. Whether that is acceptable depends on the workload.
  • This is a specialized research runtime. The result is interesting because it stretches one small system unusually far, not because it replaces mature general-purpose serving stacks.

caveats & sources

One DGX Spark, one custom work-in-progress engine, two runs for each short workload, and one random-8K run per arm. The prompts are small diagnostic slices, not standardized downstream evaluations. CB3 changes both expert availability and expert precision, so its speed and quality effects cannot be assigned to either change alone. The routing trace and held-out quality corpus are finite samples; other domains may choose different experts and pay a different penalty.
